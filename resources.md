# Resources

Curated, not comprehensive. Everything free unless marked. The list is short on purpose — with 35
hours you cannot afford to browse.

## Statistics — watch these

**StatQuest with Josh Starmer** (youtube.com/@statquest) — the single best resource for your starting
point. Short, visual, genuinely funny, and he explains the *intuition* before the formula. If you
watch nothing else, watch these, in this order:

1. "Statistics Fundamentals: Populations, Parameters and Samples"
2. "The Normal Distribution, Clearly Explained"
3. "The Central Limit Theorem, Clearly Explained"
4. "Standard Deviation vs Standard Error"
5. "p-values: What they are and how to interpret them"
6. "Bayes' Theorem, Clearly Explained"
7. "ROC and AUC, Clearly Explained"
8. "Machine Learning Fundamentals: Bias and Variance"

**3Blue1Brown** (youtube.com/@3blue1brown) — "Essence of Linear Algebra" and "Bayes theorem, the
geometry of changing beliefs." Beautiful, and worth it for the *why*. Not required.

## Statistics — read these

- **Seeing Theory** (seeing-theory.brown.edu) — interactive visual probability. Free. Best 30 minutes
  you can spend on distributions.
- **Think Stats**, Allen Downey (greenteapress.com/wp/think-stats-3e/) — free, Python-based, and
  simulation-first, which matches how these notebooks teach.
- **Introduction to Statistical Learning** (statlearning.com) — free PDF. The standard reference for
  the machine learning half. Read chapters 2–4 only; the rest is beyond this course.

## Python

- **Python for Everybody**, Chuck Severance (py4e.com) — free, patient, assumes nothing. Use it if the
  notebooks move too fast.
- **pandas "10 minutes to pandas"** (pandas.pydata.org/docs/user_guide/10min.html) — bookmark it. You
  will use it as a lookup, not a tutorial.
- Note: some tutorials predate pandas 3.0. If a snippet errors, check the official docs first.

## Ethics — the primary sources

Read these in the original. Professors notice when you have, and quoting a framework accurately is
half the battle in a discussion post.

- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0** (nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) — the spine of
  the course. Four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Know them cold.
- **EU AI Act** — read a summary of the **risk tiers** (unacceptable / high / limited / minimal), not
  the full legal text.
- **OECD AI Principles** (oecd.ai) — short. Read the actual five.
- **Fairness and Machine Learning**, Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan (fairmlbook.org) — free, and the
  technical canon. Chapters 1–3.
- **"Model Cards for Model Reporting"**, Mitchell et al. 2019 — short paper, hugely cited.
- **"Datasheets for Datasets"**, Gebru et al. 2021 — the dataset counterpart.
- **Federal Reserve SR 11-7** — model risk management guidance. You likely know it already; re-read it
  with AI governance in mind, because **mapping it to the NIST AI RMF is your differentiator.**

## Ethics — for the commute

This is where your 50 minutes a day of driving goes. Audio is the one study channel that costs you no
extra time.

- **Weapons of Math Destruction**, Cathy O'Neil (audiobook, paid) — the accessible classic. Start here.
- **The Alignment Problem**, Brian Christian (audiobook, paid) — broader and more technical; excellent.
- **Atlas of AI**, Kate Crawford (audiobook, paid) — the material/labour/environmental critique. Useful
  for the "societal impact" portion of the syllabus.
- Podcasts: *Hard Fork* (NYT), *The TWIML AI Podcast*, *Your Undivided Attention*.

## Writing and citation

- **APA 7th edition** — Purdue OWL (owl.purdue.edu) is the standard quick reference. Use
  `ethics/apa7-template.md` as your starting file.
- **Zotero** (zotero.org) — free reference manager, with a browser plugin that captures a citation in
  one click and a Word plugin that formats your bibliography. **Install it before the term starts**,
  not during your first paper. Over a 60-credit doctorate this will save you days.
- **Google Scholar** — set it to export APA citations directly.

## First week of class — an attack plan

Written now, while you are calm, for a week when you will not be.

**The day you get access:**

1. Download **both syllabi**. Read them once, fully, before doing anything else.
2. Build a single calendar of **every due date for both courses** through Dec 11. Everything. This is
   the highest-value hour of the term — an 8-week term has no slack, and a deadline you discover late
   is a deadline you miss.
3. Note the **discussion-board rhythm**. Most doctoral courses require an initial post by mid-week and
   two substantive replies by the weekend. That cadence, not the papers, is what sinks people.
4. Find the **residency weekend** date and confirm it against your work calendar.
5. Identify the **term paper** for each course and its due date. Start thinking in week 1; both courses
   reward a topic you have lived with.

**Ongoing:**

- **Post early in the week.** Early posts get more replies, which makes your own replies easier.
- **Use your bank experience deliberately.** In PhDAI 832 you have genuine authority — regulated model
  risk, fair lending, auditability. Most of your cohort is arguing from reading. You are arguing from
  practice. That is worth real marks.
- **Keep `00-notation-cheatsheet.md` open during every 730 lecture.** That is what it is for.
- **Watch the relevant StatQuest video before each 730 lecture**, not after. Ten minutes of priming
  makes an hour of lecture land differently.
